\section{Experiments}
\label{sec:experiments}

We ask three questions. (i) Does the per-input geometry of the descent beat the scalar terminal
energy---the signal Energy-Based Transformers use---as a nonconformity score? (ii) Is any lift
actually due to the \emph{restart} geometry, and which features drive it? (iii) Do the
distribution-free guarantees hold in-distribution and behave as designed under shift? A fourth,
adversarial question---does geometry beat a \emph{standard softmax-confidence} baseline?---turns
out to sharply bound the contribution, and we report it prominently.

\paragraph{Setup.} We use two structurally distinct synthetic reasoning families, each tuned to a
partially-competent $70$--$85\%$ base-accuracy regime (so selective prediction has error to remove)
with an out-of-distribution split: \emph{arithmetic} (sum classification; ID accuracy $\approx
0.81$; OOD $=$ larger operand magnitudes) and \emph{graph shortest-path} (BFS path-length
classification; ID $\approx 0.70$ over a majority-class baseline of $0.41$; OOD $=$ larger graphs).
Every method is conformalized identically, so the comparison is purely \emph{which nonconformity
score}. Baselines: the raw-energy family ($\bar E$, $E_{\min}$, energy spread) and standard
softmax-confidence scores from the decoder logits (MSP, temperature-scaled MSP fit on a disjoint
fold, predictive entropy). The learned geometry mapper is a logistic model over the $14$-feature
vector, fit on a fold \emph{disjoint} from calibration. Our primary metric is AURC (area under the
risk--coverage curve; lower is better); the falsification test is a paired-bootstrap $95\%$ CI on
$\Delta\mathrm{AURC} = \mathrm{AURC}(\text{baseline}) - \mathrm{AURC}(\text{geometry})$, reported over
$5$ seeds per task.

\paragraph{Geometry beats scalar energy (Table~\ref{tab:main}).} On both tasks the geometry mapper
beats the best raw-energy baseline on all five seeds: $\Delta\mathrm{AURC} = +0.085\pm0.036$
(arithmetic) and $+0.111\pm0.054$ (graph), every seed's CI excluding zero. The risk--coverage curves
(Fig.~\ref{fig:f2}) show geometry far below the energy scores across all coverage levels. This is the
Energy-Based-Transformer comparison, and geometry wins it decisively and reproducibly on two
structurally different tasks---so the effect is not an artifact of additive arithmetic.

\paragraph{Restarts are the mechanism (Fig.~\ref{fig:s1}).} Sweeping the number of restarts $K$, the
lift grows monotonically: at $K{=}1$---a single descent with no basin geometry, essentially the EBT
setting---geometry separates on only $1/5$ seeds ($\Delta\mathrm{AURC}=+0.026$); by $K{\ge}8$ it is
$5/5$ ($+0.083$ at $K{=}16$). The secondary falsifier (``$K{>}1$ gives no lift'') therefore does not
fire. A leave-one-group-out feature ablation (Table~\ref{tab:ablation}) localizes the source:
\emph{basin agreement} is the dominant driver (removing it nearly doubles arithmetic AURC toward the
energy level), descent dynamics helps (most on graph), and---crucially---dropping the energy
features leaves AURC essentially unchanged ($\text{drop\_energy}\approx\text{full}$, both far below
raw energy). The win over energy comes from the \emph{non-energy} geometry, not from re-using the
energy statistics the vector happens to contain. Figure~\ref{fig:f5} shows the corresponding
per-feature separation.

\paragraph{The guarantees behave as designed (Figs.~\ref{fig:f3}--\ref{fig:f6}).} In-distribution,
Learn-then-Test selective prediction is valid: achieved selective risk sits on or below the nominal
target across the $\alpha$-sweep (Fig.~\ref{fig:f3}). Conformal-Risk-Control adaptive halting
(Fig.~\ref{fig:f4}) chooses a basin-agreement threshold that uses $42\%$ of the step budget---$58\%$
compute saved---at a halting risk of $0.9\% \le \alpha{=}10\%$ with no end-task accuracy loss, and
the halting risk was empirically monotone in the threshold (CRC's assumption). Under distribution
shift the guarantee \emph{breaks by design} (Fig.~\ref{fig:f6}): the ID-calibrated threshold, applied
to OOD, achieves $76\%$ selective risk against a $10\%$ target---exchangeability is violated, so the
certificate is void. This is precisely the argument for abstention / shift detection in critical
systems: the guarantees are marginal under exchangeability, and we show them fail loudly when it
does not hold.

\paragraph{Key caveat: geometry does not beat softmax confidence (Table~\ref{tab:main}).} When the
baseline set is widened from scalar energy to a standard softmax-confidence score (MSP,
temperature-scaled MSP, predictive entropy), the broader claim does \emph{not} survive. Against the
best of \emph{all} baselines, $\Delta\mathrm{AURC} = -0.011\pm0.006$ on arithmetic ($0/5$ seeds
favor geometry) and $-0.034\pm0.008$ on graph ($0/5$): softmax confidence \emph{ties} geometry on
arithmetic and \emph{beats} it on graph. In Fig.~\ref{fig:f2}, geometry, MSP, and entropy overlap at
the bottom, all far below the energy scores. We therefore state the contribution precisely:
\textbf{inference-time landscape geometry beats the EBT scalar-energy signal, but is not a better
nonconformity score than a well-calibrated softmax}---under this reasoner. The likely reason is the
reasoner itself: a supervised basin-center energy with a shallow decoder makes the softmax already
informative, leaving little for geometry to add beyond energy. The distribution-free certificate
machinery---selective prediction, adaptive halting, and their in-distribution validity and
out-of-distribution breakage---holds regardless of which score wins.

\paragraph{The landscape was the limiter (learned-landscape reasoner).} We test that explanation
directly by replacing the supervised basin-center energy with a genuinely learned, input-conditioned
multi-basin landscape---per-class latent anchors trained as attractors by a contrastive-plus-%
stationarity objective (pushing the correct anchor below competitors and driving
$\|\nabla_z E(\mu_y)\|\!\to\!0$ so it is a local minimum), with annealed Langevin descent at
inference. This reasoner reasons (arithmetic ID $0.72$--$0.90$), and on \emph{arithmetic} geometry \emph{does}
beat the best softmax-confidence baseline: over $5$ seeds, $\Delta\mathrm{AURC} = +0.007\pm0.005$
against the best overall baseline ($4/5$ seeds' CI excluding zero) and $+0.159\pm0.051$ against
scalar energy---compared to $-0.011$ ($0/5$) under the basin-center reasoner. So a genuinely
multi-basin landscape can flip the caveat on arithmetic: inference-time geometry can beat a
well-calibrated softmax when the task supports it. The effect is, however, \emph{task-dependent}, and
not simply a matter of reasoner accuracy. On \emph{graph}, a first (capacity-limited) IRED reasoner
only tied softmax; the natural explanation was its weakness, so we tested it with a larger IRED graph
reasoner (hidden $256$, latent $48$) that reaches ID $\approx0.83$---as strong as arithmetic. It
\emph{still} only ties: over $5$ seeds $\Delta\mathrm{AURC}=+0.001\pm0.003$ against the best softmax
baseline ($0/5$), while still beating scalar energy $5/5$. So the flip is specific to arithmetic, not
a general property of learned landscapes nor an artifact of accuracy: on graph, a well-calibrated
softmax already captures what the descent geometry would add. We report this narrowly and read it as
a boundary on when landscape geometry is worth its cost.

\paragraph{Redundancy vs.\ complementarity (Table~\ref{tab:complementarity}).} ``Ties softmax'' is
ambiguous: geometry could carry signal softmax lacks yet not enough to win alone. We resolve it by
fitting a nonconformity mapper on $[\text{geometry}\cup\text{softmax}]$ and comparing it to one on
$[\text{softmax}]$ alone (both on the fit fold); positive $\Delta\mathrm{AURC}$ means geometry adds
conditional signal. Across all four (task, reasoner) cells, geometry adds over softmax \emph{only}
where it also wins head-to-head---arithmetic-IRED ($4/5$, $+0.007$). In every other cell, including
graph-IRED, the combined mapper is no better than softmax alone ($\Delta\mathrm{AURC}\approx+0.001$,
$0/5$). So the graph tie is \emph{redundancy}, not complementarity: a well-calibrated softmax already
captures whatever the descent geometry would contribute there. The clean summary is that inference
geometry always beats the EBT scalar energy, and beats or complements softmax only under a learned
landscape on arithmetic.

\paragraph{Richer geometry: task property, not thin features (Table~\ref{tab:richer}).} The
redundancy verdict could be an artifact of an impoverished curvature description---our base features
summarise the latent Hessian only by $\lambda_{\max}$ and $\mathrm{tr}(H)$. We test this by adding two
richer feature groups and re-running the complementarity analysis: the \emph{full} Hessian spectrum
(smallest eigenvalue, negative-eigenvalue fraction, effective rank, log-determinant; the exact
$d\times d$ eigendecomposition, since $d$ is small) and \emph{mode connectivity} (energy barriers
along the path between restart basins). Across all four cells the richer geometry changes nothing: on
the crux graph-IRED cell the conditional signal over softmax stays at $-0.002$ ($0/5$); it is a wash
where geometry already wins (arithmetic-IRED, $+0.007$, $4/5$, unchanged) and slightly \emph{worse} on
the fixed-bowl basin-center landscapes, where extra features only add noise. So more landscape signal
does not help on graph---the graph redundancy is a property of the task, not of thin features, and a
well-calibrated softmax already captures what even the richest descent geometry would add.
